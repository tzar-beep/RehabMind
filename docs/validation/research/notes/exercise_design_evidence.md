# Evidence base for picture-naming and sentence-level exercise design in post-stroke aphasia (RehabMind)

Scope: what the literature says about stimulus difficulty, cueing/feedback, dosage, generalisation and sentence/discourse treatment, translated into implementable recommendations for RehabMind (61 picture stimuli, word-tile sentence building, scene description, typed/spoken answers, hand-assigned difficulty 1-5, "meaning first, then first sound" hints, LLM-written prompts/hints/feedback within clinician limits). Research date: Oct 2026. Evidence strength flagged as: [SR/MA] systematic review / meta-analysis, [RCT], [SSED] single-subject / case series, [Consensus].

## 1. Which psycholinguistic variables predict naming difficulty, and which free normative datasets exist?

### Takeaway
Age of acquisition (AoA), name agreement, imageability/concreteness and length are better-supported predictors of aphasic naming difficulty than raw word frequency (whose effect shrinks once correlated variables are controlled); visual complexity contributes little. Predictors vary by individual, so a fixed hand-assigned 1-5 difficulty should be replaced or supplemented by a score computed from published norms plus per-patient calibration. Good free norms exist for English (Kuperman AoA, Brysbaert concreteness, Glasgow Norms, MultiPic/MultiPic-32, BOSS photos); no Hindi picture-naming norms for aphasia were found.

### Cited Findings
- Nickels & Howard (1995, Neuropsychologia 33:1281-1303) examined AoA, operativity, frequency, familiarity, imageability, concreteness, length and visual complexity of the picture across two groups of people with aphasia [SSED/case series, group + individual analyses] — [Macquarie: Aphasic naming: what matters?](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/aphasic-naming-what-matters/)
- They found "remarkably small" effects of word frequency once intercorrelated variables were controlled; many patients still showed AoA effects after frequency and familiarity were accounted for; operativity, imageability and length predicted naming for some patients; visual complexity did not — [Macquarie](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/aphasic-naming-what-matters/); [Newcastle eprints](https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/33785)
- Crucially, the two groups showed different predictor patterns and individuals' predictors differed from the group's — [Macquarie](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/aphasic-naming-what-matters/)
- Kittredge, Dell, Verkuilen & Schwartz (2008, Cognitive Neuropsychology 25:463-492) studied where frequency acts in aphasic picture naming errors (PNT data); abstract not retrieved in this session — [citation via search results](https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/33785)
- Name agreement (degree to which people agree on a picture's name) is a strong predictor of picture naming in healthy adults; Bose & Schafer (2017) compared high- vs low-agreement images in 10 people with aphasia and 21 matched controls (results not retrieved — page blocked) — [Reading CentAUR: Name agreement in aphasia](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/68382/)
- A British norming study on the Snodgrass & Vanderwart set found name frequency, the AoA x frequency interaction, and name agreement were the main determinants of naming speed (healthy speakers) — [search summary of Snodgrass-Vanderwart norms](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1080/783663595)
- The theoretical rationale that later-acquired words are lost first in aphasia, and that images should be unequivocally nameable to be reliable stimuli, is stated in Italian picture-norm work — [Sapienza IRIS](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/629595)
- Datasets:
  - Kuperman, Stadthagen-Gonzalez & Brysbaert (2012, Behav Res Methods 44:978-990): AoA ratings for 30,121 English content words via MTurk, reliability comparable to lab norms; supplementary XLSX added by 2013 erratum. No explicit licence found — [Erratum record](https://mijn.bsl.nl/article/10.3758/s13428-013-0348-8); [McMaster](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/57348)
  - Brysbaert, Warriner & Kuperman (2014): concreteness for 37,058 English words + 2,896 two-word expressions, >4,000 raters. No explicit licence found — [McMaster](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/57343)
  - Glasgow Norms (Scott et al., 2019, Behav Res Methods 51:1258-1270): 5,553 English words on nine scales (arousal, valence, dominance, concreteness, imageability, familiarity, AoA, semantic size, gender association); 379 ambiguous words with sense disambiguation; open-access version under Creative Commons Attribution — [PMC6538586](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6538586/); [Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/165799)
  - MultiPic (Duñabeitia et al., 2018, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology): 750 coloured line drawings normed for name agreement and visual complexity in British English, Spanish, French, Dutch, Italian, German; stimuli and norms freely available at bcbl.eu/databases/multipic — [Reading CentAUR](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/68990); [MPI](https://forms.mpi.nl/node/46373)
  - Multilingual Picture Database (2022, Scientific Data): 500 MultiPic pictures with highest cross-language name agreement, extended to 32 languages/varieties (incl. American English, Mandarin, Korean, Welsh; Hindi NOT included); provides H statistic (higher = more divergent naming), modal response %, "don't know" %, idiosyncratic %, familiarity; CSV/XLSX plus B/W and greyscale drawings on figshare DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.19328939.v5; article is CC BY 4.0 (dataset licence should be checked on figshare) — [PMC9304413](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9304413)
  - BOSS (Brodeur et al., 2010 PLoS ONE; Phase II 2014 PLoS ONE): normed colour photos of objects (Phase I described as 480 or 538 photos; Phase II adds 930), norms for name, familiarity, visual complexity, object agreement, viewpoint agreement, manipulability; one listing gives licence as CC BY-SA (unconfirmed); extensions include Dutch names for 1,400 photos and Brazilian/Russian norms — [PMC4161371](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4161371/); [cogsci.nl stimulus sets](https://cogsci.nl/stimulus-sets); [MPI Dutch BOSS](https://forms.mpi.nl/node/54028)
  - Hindi: only a Hindi-English bilingual AoA/frequency norming + naming study (college students, not aphasia) was found; predictor-outcome relationships were broadly similar to English monolinguals — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/86k9s280)

### Inferences
- RehabMind's photos are closest to BOSS (photos) and its line drawings to MultiPic. Mapping each of the 61 stimuli to its MultiPic/BOSS entry gives name-agreement (H, modal %) and visual complexity; mapping the target word to Kuperman AoA, Brysbaert concreteness, Glasgow imageability/familiarity, and SUBTLEX-US Zipf frequency gives the lexical side.
- Implementable difficulty score (replace or audit hand-assigned 1-5): z-score and combine, weighting AoA, name agreement (H) and imageability/concreteness most, length (phonemes/syllables) next, frequency (Zipf) lower, visual complexity lowest; bin into quintiles to produce 1-5. Then report agreement (e.g., Spearman rho / weighted kappa) between hand-assigned and norm-derived levels as a first "validation" artefact for the industry reviewer.
- Exclude or flag stimuli with low name agreement (e.g., modal % below roughly 70-80% or high H) because a "wrong" answer may be a valid alternative name; accept listed alternative names (from norms' response distributions) as correct in scoring. Thresholds are a design choice, not sourced.
- Because predictors differ across individuals (Nickels & Howard), adapt per patient: estimate item difficulty from the patient's own accuracy history (e.g., simple Rasch/logistic model) seeded with the norm-based prior.
- For Hindi, norms would need to be collected locally (e.g., re-norming MultiPic pictures in Hindi with name-agreement and AoA ratings) — no off-the-shelf resource found.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes (odds ratios / beta weights) per variable in large aphasia samples (e.g., Kittredge et al. 2008; Bose & Schafer 2017 results) were not retrieved.
- Licences for SUBTLEX-US, Kuperman AoA, Brysbaert concreteness, Snodgrass-Vanderwart (and its colourised Rossion & Pourtois version), MultiPic and BOSS were not confirmed from official pages; must be checked before redistribution (commonly "free for research" rather than a formal open licence). SUBTLEX-US details (Brysbaert & New 2009, Zipf scale) were not verified in this session.
- No published Hindi aphasia naming norms or Hindi picture name-agreement norms located.

## 2. Cueing hierarchies, SFA, PCA, errorless vs errorful learning — implications for hints and feedback

### Takeaway
Semantic Feature Analysis (SFA) and Phonological Components Analysis (PCA) reliably improve naming of trained items, with limited generalisation; errorless (decreasing-cue) and errorful (increasing-cue) approaches give broadly equivalent naming gains, with individual cognitive profile mattering more than method. RehabMind's "meaning first, then first sound" increasing-cue hierarchy is defensible, but feedback after each attempt should be retained and the system should offer a low-error (decreasing-cue / model-then-repeat) mode for patients with poorer memory/monitoring.

### Cited Findings
- SFA meta-analysis (Oh et al., 2016; 11 studies): medium-to-large effect sizes for trained items, small for untrained items; limited generalisation to untrained items, discourse and standardised naming tests [SR/MA] — [Ewha Pure](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/treatment-efficacy-of-semantic-feature-analyses-for-persons-with-/)
- Maddy et al. (2014) systematic review (1980-June 2013): SFA effective for confrontation naming in nondegenerative aphasia, but many studies reported limited generalisation to untrained items and connected speech [SR] — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/68f09949-63e1-4827-af5b-f808453c410c)
- Efstratiadou et al. (2018) systematic review of 21 SFA studies: effective, limited generalisation to untreated items; may work better in mild-moderate fluent aphasia [SR] — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/19317)
- Kendall et al. (2019) RCT-style comparison: SFA and phonomotor treatment generalised to untrained words sharing semantic or phonological features with trained items, but not to unrelated untrained words — [Kendall Phonomotor 2019](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstream/2263/75004/1/Kendall_Phonomotor_2019.pdf)
- PCA (Leonard, Rochon & Laird, 2008, Aphasiology): modelled on SFA, the person names a picture and generates five phonological components (e.g., rhyme, first sound, last sound, first sound associate, syllable count); efficacious for naming across several research groups; one participant (jargon aphasia) did not improve [SSED] — [EasyChair preprint](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/mmlJ); [Frontiers abstract](https://www.frontiersin.org/10.3389/conf.fpsyg.2015.65.00062/event_abstract)
- Active participant choice of phonological attributes within PCA was explored as a factor in outcome — [U Toronto](https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/items/dc9f6715-bb9c-4383-9453-08ef6cfea419)
- Errorless vs errorful: a 2003 review of anomia studies found equivalent rate of success; errorless approaches gave positive immediate effects but long-term effects/generalisation often unreported; too few studies for formal meta-analysis — [Manchester: application of errorless learning to aphasic disorders](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/the-application-of-errorless-learning-to-aphasic-disorders-a-revi/)
- Fillingham et al.: errorless and errorful therapy equivalent immediately and at follow-up, even without therapist feedback; better responders to both had better recognition memory, executive/problem-solving and monitoring skills — [Manchester: errorless vs errorful, frontal executive skills](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/treatment-of-anomia-using-errorless-vs-errorful-learning-are-fron/); [PMC4105211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4105211)
- Contrasting result: McKissock & Ward found errorful training without feedback led to no improvement in picture naming (i.e., feedback matters when errors are allowed) — [PMC4105211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4105211)
- Conroy, Sage & Lambon Ralph (2009): errorless (decreasing-cue) therapy for verb and noun naming was as effective as errorful/hierarchical cueing and more time-efficient — [Manchester](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/errorless-and-errorful-therapy-for-verb-and-noun-naming-in-aphasi/)
- Increasing cue hierarchy = start with least support (errorful); decreasing = start with maximal support and fade (errorless) — [UWO EBP brief](https://www.uwo.ca/fhs/lwm/teaching/EBP/2006_07/Angle.pdf)
- Items named accurately after therapy needed significantly shorter phonemic cues before therapy (Conroy, Snell, Sage & Lambon Ralph, 2012) — i.e., pre-therapy cue-responsiveness predicts item learnability — [Manchester/Sheffield Hallam](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/11614/)
- Abel et al. (2005, Aphasiology) "Decreasing and increasing cues in naming therapy for aphasia" exists; results not retrieved — [citation via deepdyve result](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/doc/EDOGVWHaZu)

### Inferences
- Keep the semantic-then-phonological increasing hierarchy as default: (1) open semantic prompt / SFA-style feature questions (category, use, location, properties), (2) sentence-completion / associate cue, (3) first phoneme/grapheme, (4) first syllable, (5) whole word model + repetition. Always end every trial with the correct word presented and a repetition/typing attempt so no trial ends on an error (reduces error reinforcement concern).
- Provide a clinician toggle "errorless mode" (start at step 5 and fade cues across sessions) for patients with poor monitoring/memory, consistent with Fillingham's individual-difference findings.
- Log the cue level at which each item is produced. "Number of cues needed" is a more sensitive, clinically familiar metric than binary accuracy and can drive item difficulty updates; Conroy 2012 suggests short-phonemic-cue items are the most learnable, so prioritise them early.
- LLM-generated hints should be constrained to SFA feature slots (category, function, location, property, association) and PCA slots (first sound, rhyme, syllables) with templated validation (e.g., hint must not contain the target word or a morphological variant; first-sound hint must match the target's actual phoneme, not letter — "phone" starts with /f/). These are concrete, testable constraints for a validation suite.
- Feedback: give immediate, explicit knowledge-of-results plus correct model; avoid uncontrolled praise for incorrect responses. Errorful practice without feedback appears ineffective (McKissock & Ward).

### Gaps
- Direct head-to-head results for increasing vs decreasing cue hierarchies (Abel et al. 2005; Best et al.) not retrieved.
- No systematic review found of cueing within computer-delivered self-managed therapy specifically.

## 3. Dosage and intensity (RELEASE, Big CACTUS, FCET2EC, Cochrane)

### Takeaway
Cochrane and RELEASE support that more therapy helps, with best outcomes around 20-50 total hours, delivered frequently (3-5+ days/week), with home practice; FCET2EC showed >=10 h/week for 3 weeks improves everyday communication in chronic aphasia; Big CACTUS showed ~28 hours of self-managed computer practice over 6 months substantially improved naming of personally relevant words but not conversation. High intensity raises dropout, so RehabMind should track adherence and allow flexible session lengths.

### Cited Findings
- Brady et al. (2016) Cochrane review (CD000425.pub4): SLT vs no SLT improved functional communication (SMD 0.28, 95% CI 0.06-0.49; 27 RCTs, 1,620 participants), reading, writing and expressive language; benefit not evident at follow-up on smaller numbers [SR/MA] — [UCC CORA](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/6604); [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/d095f5f2-387e-4dc1-a063-236c32368c4a)
- Same review (38 comparisons): high intensity, high dose or longer-duration SLT gave better functional communication, confounded by significantly higher dropout; may not be acceptable to all [SR/MA] — [UCC CORA](https://cora.ucc.ie/handle/10468/6604)
- RELEASE (2022, Stroke) network meta-analysis of individual participant data (25 trials, 959 participants): greatest gains with >20-50 total hours; 2-4 and >=9 hours/week; 3-5+ days/week for overall language and functional communication; no comprehension gains with <=20 h, <3 h/week, <=3 days/week [SR/IPD NMA, observational across trials] — [Edinburgh research portal](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/dosage-intensity-and-frequency-of-language-therapy-for-aphasia-a-/); [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/29362/); [MIMS summary](https://www.mims.com/india/news-updates/topic/50-hour-speech-and-language-therapy-best-bet-for-poststroke-aphasia)
- RELEASE subgroups: working-age, chronic, mild-moderate participants gained most with high frequency/intensity; older, moderate-severe participants within 3 months of onset gained most with lower intensity; best regimens combined receptive and expressive tasks, functional goals and prescribed home practice — [MIMS summary](https://www.mims.com/india/news-updates/topic/50-hour-speech-and-language-therapy-best-bet-for-poststroke-aphasia); [Bond research portal](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/dosage-intensity-and-frequency-of-language-therapy-for-aphasia-a-/)
- FCET2EC (Breitenstein et al., 2017, Lancet 389:1528-1538): 156 people with chronic aphasia (1-6 years post-stroke, mean age 53) randomised to immediate intensive SLT vs 3-week wait; intervention at least 10 h/week over 3 weeks (one summary: 22 h individual + 9 h group + 15 h self-led); verbal communication improved, stable at 6 months; controls did not improve [RCT] — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01540383](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01540383); [NIHR Evidence](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/intensive-speech-therapy-helps-stroke-survivors-with-persistent-communication-difficulties); [MedicalResearch interview](https://medicalresearch.com/intensive-therapy-facilitates-speech-recovery-in-chronic-aphasia-after-stroke/)
- Big CACTUS (Palmer et al., 2019, Lancet Neurology 18:821-833): 278 participants with chronic post-stroke aphasia; self-managed, individually tailored computerised word-finding practice daily for 6 months; word finding of personally relevant words improved 16.2 percentage points vs usual care (95% CI 12.7-19.6) and 14.4 vs attention control (puzzle book); no conversation benefit (TOMs difference -0.03, 95% CI -0.21 to 0.14) [RCT] — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/149577)
- Big CACTUS practice: average 28 hours over 6 months vs 3.8 hours usual therapy; gains maintained at least 6 months; possibly cost-effective for mild-moderate word-finding difficulty — [NIHR Evidence](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/computerised-speech-and-language-therapy-can-help-people-with-aphasia-find-words-following-a-stroke); [Sheffield CTRU](https://sheffield.ac.uk/ctru/impact/stroke)

### Inferences
- Default dosage targets for RehabMind: clinician-configurable weekly goal with a default in the 2-4 h/week band over 5 days/week (e.g., 25-45 min/day), with a visible cumulative-hours counter toward ~20-50 h per block. Allow higher intensity (>=9 h/week) for chronic mild-moderate users, lower for acute/severe/older users per RELEASE subgroups.
- Log actual practice time, trials completed and dropout/adherence — the Cochrane dropout confound means adherence metrics are part of clinical validation.
- Big CACTUS is the closest analogue (self-managed computer naming practice): its primary outcome (naming of personally relevant treated words in picture naming) and its null conversation result define realistic claims: RehabMind can plausibly target item-level naming gains, not conversation, without extra components.
- A within-session success-rate target (often described clinically as ~70-80% to balance challenge and errors) is NOT sourced here; treat as a configurable parameter and avoid citing it as evidence.

### Gaps
- No sourced evidence found for optimal trials per session or target success rate per session.
- Big CACTUS software details (StepByStep, cue types, volunteer support, how many words) not verified in this session.
- Brady et al. updated Cochrane review (2025, if published) not checked.

## 4. Generalisation to untrained items and functional communication; implications for item selection

### Takeaway
Naming therapy reliably improves trained items; generalisation to untrained items is small and mostly limited to items sharing semantic/phonological features, and transfer to conversation is weak (Big CACTUS null). Item selection should therefore prioritise personally relevant, functional vocabulary as treatment targets, and the app must keep separate untrained probe lists to measure (not assume) generalisation.

### Cited Findings
- Small effect sizes for untrained items in SFA meta-analysis; limited generalisation to discourse and standardised tests [SR/MA] — [Ewha Pure](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/treatment-efficacy-of-semantic-feature-analyses-for-persons-with-/)
- Generalisation only to untrained words sharing semantic or phonological features with trained words — [Kendall 2019](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstream/2263/75004/1/Kendall_Phonomotor_2019.pdf)
- Big CACTUS outcome was personally relevant word retrieval; conversation unchanged; authors call for work on generalising new vocabulary to conversation [RCT] — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/149577)
- Conroy and colleagues studied whether picture-naming gains generalise to connected speech ("Improved vocabulary production after naming therapy in aphasia: can gains in picture naming generalise to connected speech?") — [UWE repository](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/index.php/output/989907/improved-vocabulary-production-after-naming-therapy-in-aphasia-can-gains-in-picture-naming-generalise-to-connected-speech)
- Aphasia United Best Practice Recommendations (Simmons-Mackie, Worrall et al., 2017, Aphasiology 31:131-151): multinational consensus on screening, assessment, intervention and discharge; includes communication partner training and that no one should leave services without a way to communicate needs [Consensus] — [ASHA Evidence Map: Top Ten](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/77be5394-55a3-4085-b36b-81b060fc8a7a); [Aphasia-friendly English version](https://aphasiatrials.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Aphasia-United-BPR-English-Aphasia-Friendly.pdf)
- RELEASE: best outcomes when therapy is tailored to functional goals and includes home practice — [MIMS summary](https://www.mims.com/india/news-updates/topic/50-hour-speech-and-language-therapy-best-bet-for-poststroke-aphasia)

### Inferences
- Implement three item lists per patient: Treated set (trained), Untrained-related probe set (semantically/phonologically related to treated items, never cued or given feedback), Untrained-unrelated control set. Probe at baseline (>=3 baselines for stability), every N sessions, post-block and follow-up — this mirrors single-case experimental design and gives the clinician a defensible effect measure (e.g., Tau-U or % change) on the dashboard.
- Allow clinicians/patients to add personal photos/words (family names, local foods, places); compute norms where available and fall back to patient-calibrated difficulty for idiosyncratic items.
- Add a bridging step from naming to use: after correct naming, prompt a short sentence using the word ("Tell me something you do with a kettle") to push toward connected speech; report this separately and do not claim conversational gains without measurement.
- Pre-test items and select those named with a short phonemic cue but not spontaneously ("in the zone") as treatment targets (Conroy 2012).

### Gaps
- Best-practice statements specifically on personally relevant item selection not retrieved; Aphasia United full text not read.
- No quantitative data on how many trained items per set is optimal.

## 5. Sentence-level and discourse treatments (VNeST, TUF, RET) relevant to word-tile sentences and scene description

### Takeaway
Verb-centred treatments (VNeST), Treatment of Underlying Forms (TUF, guided by the Complexity Account of Treatment Efficacy) and Response Elaboration Training (RET) have the strongest sentence/discourse evidence, mostly from single-subject designs plus one Bayesian meta-analysis for TUF. RehabMind's word-tile and scene-description tasks should be restructured around verbs and thematic roles (who does what to whom), complexity-graded sentence types, and elaboration of the patient's own utterances, scored with CIU-type measures.

### Cited Findings
- VNeST (Edmonds & Kiran, 2006): 4 participants; training retrieval of agent-patient pairs for trained verbs (e.g., measure: chef/sugar, surveyor/land), 2x/week; generalisation to sentence production in picture description for trained and semantically related untrained verbs in all participants [SSED] — [Pitt Aphasiology Archive](https://aphasiology.pitt.edu/1734/); [UT repository](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/9667)
- 2024 trial: VNeST alone improved trained verb naming and more complete utterances in discourse at 1 week; generalisation to untrained verbs, sentence production and comprehension was significantly greater with real tDCS than sham [RCT, small] — [PMC11745310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11745310/)
- TUF/CATE: training complex structures (e.g., wh-movement, filler-gap sentences) generalises to less complex sentences sharing processes (Thompson et al., 2003) [SSED] — [PMC1995234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1995234); [CATE paper](https://www.bu.edu/aphasiaresearch/files/2009/11/CATE-paper.pdf)
- 2021 Bayesian meta-analysis of TUF (13 single-subject multiple-baseline studies, 46 people with aphasia): generalisable group-level efficacy, results consistent with CATE; moderators were severity and time post-onset [MA of SSEDs] — [JSLHR 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-21-00131)
- RET (Kearns, 1985 onwards): elicits the speaker's own response to action pictures, then clinician models and forward-chains to expand utterance length and content; 17 people with aphasia (Broca's, anomic, conduction) showed positive effects across RET studies; strong response generalisation to similar untrained pictures, stimulus generalisation less studied [SSED] — [Pitt Aphasiology Archive RET](https://aphasiology.pitt.edu/2364/); [AJSLP 2013](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2013/12-0063))
- Modified RET for procedural discourse/personal recounts (Wambaugh et al.): outcome measured as correct information units (CIUs); CIU increases in 11 of 12 applications across 6 participants with chronic aphasia — [AJSLP 2018](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0152); [Utah collections](https://collections.lib.utah.edu/details?id=712571)
- Research dosages for RET exceed standard clinical practice — [Pitt Aphasiology Archive 2440](https://aphasiology.pitt.edu/2440/)

### Inferences
- Word-tile sentence builder: organise items around a target verb with agent and patient slots (VNeST structure: "Who can [verb]? What do they [verb]?"), then assemble the full sentence. Grade by syntactic complexity (SV -> SVO -> SVO+adjunct -> passive / object-relative / wh-question) per CATE; let clinicians choose whether to start complex (CATE) or simple.
- Distractor tiles should be controlled (e.g., role-reversal foils for reversible sentences, semantically related verbs) — makes errors diagnostic.
- Scene description: adopt RET-style elaboration — accept the patient's initial utterance, LLM proposes one expansion question at a time ("Who is doing it?", "Where?", "Why?"), patient repeats the expanded sentence. Score with CIUs, CIUs/min and % complete utterances (main concept/verb-argument completeness) rather than free LLM judgement; compute these from Whisper transcripts with documented rules.
- LLM text must stay within short, simple syntax and avoid introducing new vocabulary beyond the clinician-set level; validate with automatic readability/length checks.

### Gaps
- No systematic review of VNeST found in this session (individual studies only); no RCT-level evidence for RET or TUF.
- No evidence found on digital/word-tile (receptive assembly) versions of sentence treatments vs spoken production; tile-ordering is closer to sentence-anagram / mapping-therapy tasks whose evidence was not retrieved.
- Hindi syntax (SOV, postpositions, case marking) would require redesigned tile templates; no Hindi sentence-treatment evidence located.
